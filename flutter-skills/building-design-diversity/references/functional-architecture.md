# From function to a spatial idea

Conclusion: design a useful spatial relationship before selecting a roof costume. This is a visual concept method, not an engineering code or an invented simulation specification.

Identify who approaches, what happens in the dominant space, what must be stored/sheltered/displayed, and what public/private or open/closed relationship makes the purpose visible. Use only project-established activities as facts; label illustrative assumptions. Sketch an adjacency idea, then a plan and a simple section through the main activity. The section catches roof heights and equipment that a footprint misses.

Translate activity into masses: a long shared work surface may suggest a bay; audiences suggest oriented stage/seating space; teaching can suggest a main room and smaller service spaces; a ceremonial focus can organize a central chamber or a sequence of thresholds. None implies a compulsory gameplay mechanic or historically unique layout.

Derive roof, openings and access from those masses. Decide which volumes need span/height, where a canopy meets a wall and what holds up a feature. Allow solid/open alternation and meaningful gaps; a recognizable negative space can distinguish a building without a new tower on every design.

For a family of concepts, vary spatial organization as needed: linear bay, court, clustered wings, tall core with low support rooms, hall with frontage, pavilion or terraced complex. They are a vocabulary, not a quota. Do not force every race/function into one member of the list.

Readability comes from the intended approach and roof-on gameplay view. An elaborate interior may be invisible under an opaque roof; give the exterior a legitimate expression of its activity if required. Do not open a wall or detach a roof solely to expose props unless the asset brief calls for that view.

When a promising silhouette has an impossible joint, preserve its design goal while repairing supports/planes. Do not automatically fall back to the reference house. Inspect the revised design in the same camera and compare with the nearest existing building.
